By James O'Donnell • 9.21.26

*Welcome back to The Algorithm! *

It’s been a little while since I wrote this newsletter. Last month, I
withdrew into a bit of a writing cave to finally finish a story that had
been in the works for some time. A lot happened while I was sequestered
away. Existential panic about AI has gone mainstream
<https://us.list-manage.com/85PaRTjvxGk?e=68029f7c7c&c2id=a2f1a2f2b49af1668857eb9c317520c6>.
Mathematicians are becoming depressed
<https://us.list-manage.com/ayl5srxomqV?e=68029f7c7c&c2id=a2f1a2f2b49af1668857eb9c317520c6>
about the technology’s impact on their field. AI leaders are calling for a
slowdown
<https://us.list-manage.com/K8fu17tcU2f?e=68029f7c7c&c2id=a2f1a2f2b49af1668857eb9c317520c6>
.

But today, I’m proud to share our investigation with you. With my colleague
Eileen Guo, I spent much of the last 15 months looking at the technology
being deployed at the US-Mexico border. I’m going to keep this newsletter
brief, because I hope you will just go read our story. But here’s a quick
primer.
<https://us.list-manage.com/AMCR2o27aSf?e=68029f7c7c&c2id=a2f1a2f2b49af1668857eb9c317520c6>

Our focus was the growing “virtual wall” of more than 800 surveillance
towers that the US has spent 25 years and billions of dollars building,
with political support from both parties.

Increasingly powered by AI, it promises to make the border more secure by
enabling faster responses to crossings, and even to save the lives of
migrants.

But last summer I was looking at a map
<https://us.list-manage.com/15sngxCI7a9?e=68029f7c7c&c2id=a2f1a2f2b49af1668857eb9c317520c6>
of these towers while also seeing some headlines about people who had
recently died of heatstroke crossing the border. On a whim, I compared the
locations of deaths in New Mexico with the locations of towers and noticed
something peculiar: Some of the deaths had occurred right near the towers.
Why couldn’t such sophisticated surveillance catch people before they died?
And what else is it missing?

*That sent us on a journey to compile data on nearly 4,000 migrant deaths
and map them against generations of these surveillance towers.* It’s the
first analysis to do so. We also spoke to more than 45 sources, from Border
Patrol agents to volunteers to presidential advisors. I took trips to
multiple sections of the border, shadowing search and rescue groups in the
New Mexico desert and going on ride-alongs with sheriffs in south Texas.

The resulting story shows that the ongoing humanitarian crisis at the
border, which has gone on for decades, is unfolding more visibly than
previously understood. It also reveals flaws in the virtual wall just as
it’s set to get an unprecedented expansion—Customs and Border Protection
(CBP) plans to spend $1 billion to triple the number of towers by 2034.

*But I think it also says something about AI.*

The fear of AI discussed often today is a cataclysmic one. At its core is a
worry AI might get so powerful that humans cease to be able to control it.

But reading our investigation, you might come away with an almost opposite
fear: AI can make a complex problem look simpler than it is—and make it
harder to tell whether technology is actually working.

CBP’s journey with AI started earlier than most—it was piloting autonomous
surveillance towers from the defense tech company Anduril in 2018. The
pitch was that these towers could watch the border and alert agents only if
they needed to act.

The agency started to make bolder promises about this autonomous technology
than any other tech that came before it. Having AI watch the border would
mean fewer agents monitoring video feeds, more agents in the field, better
information about what they were walking into—and even migrant lives saved.

By many accounts, the autonomy helped: Agents we spoke with said the
technology would alert them to people crossing before they saw them. The
problem, as our investigation details, is that sometimes these promises
were unmet with tragic consequences. Sometimes the technology failed to
detect. Sometimes agents failed to respond.

It was hardly the first technology with flaws. But the more people I spoke
with, the more it seemed that the promise of humanlike perception had
raised expectations for what it could do to the point that the agency felt
less compelled—or even less able—to measure its performance. We estimate
that more than 110 people have died near the agency’s autonomous systems
(along with others who died near legacy systems without AI capability), but
we found that when a body is discovered, CBP does not formally investigate
whether surveillance should have detected someone or, if they were
detected, why agents didn’t reach them before they died.

*So yes, fear the drone swarms and bioweapons and agentic cybercrime, of
course. *But keep an eye on another side of AI risk: the possibility that
misplaced faith in AI’s capabilities lowers the standards of judgment and
oversight applied to it.

*What’s at stake in AI’s trillion-dollar gamble *

A handful of so-called hyperscalers—Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, and
Oracle—are poised to spend about $750 billion this year on building new
data centers, growing to $1.1 trillion through 2027. The reality is that
it’s a giant gamble. This story by my colleague David Rotman explains what
it would take for it to pay off (and what happens if it doesn’t). (MIT

*An AI hallucination nearly triggered a US military operation*

An intelligence report this spring in the midst of US strikes on Iran
immediately set off alarm bells: It claimed a Chinese ship in the Middle
East was transporting components of a nuclear weapons program. The US
military got planes into the air to respond and started readying personnel
to board the ship. But when officials dug deeper, they found it was
“entirely false.” (CNN

*Gemini has carried out Google’s first known AI hack*

Back in May, Google’s Gemini model accessed the internet and hacked other
companies during tests run by a small Israeli cybersecurity startup called
Irregular, which was also involved in similar incidents disclosed by
OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta. Google said it didn’t disclose the hacks at
the time because the model ended each intrusion after determining it had
accessed a real company’s systems. (WSJ
<https://us.list-manage.com/6DF6WG1WKS5?e=68029f7c7c&c2id=a2f1a2f2b49af1668857eb9c317520c6>
)

*OpenAI and Microsoft execs agree with AI’s critics*

Large language models are built on the “largest theft of labor in human
history” and are killing the web, according to executives working on AI at
Microsoft and OpenAI. The admissions were made public last Thursday in
documents that had been sealed or redacted at the request of the companies,
as part of the long-running copyright lawsuit *New York Times v. OpenAI*.
