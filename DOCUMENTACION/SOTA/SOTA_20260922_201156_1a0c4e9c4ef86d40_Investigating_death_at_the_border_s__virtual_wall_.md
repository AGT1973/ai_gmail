Welcome back to The Algorithm!

It’s been a little while since I wrote this newsletter. Last month, I withdrew into a bit of a writing cave to finally finish a story that had been in the works for some time. A lot happened while I was sequestered away. Existential panic about AI has gone mainstream (https://ter.li/ulw43iit?mc_cid=6b156c1050&mc_eid=UNIQID) . Mathematicians are becoming depressed (https://ter.li/b62dbu1r?mc_cid=6b156c1050&mc_eid=UNIQID) about the technology’s impact on their field. AI leaders are calling for a slowdown (https://ter.li/opgrvqd5?mc_cid=6b156c1050&mc_eid=UNIQID) .

But today, I’m proud to share our investigation with you. With my colleague Eileen Guo, I spent much of the last 15 months looking at the technology being deployed at the US-Mexico border. I’m going to keep this newsletter brief, because I hope you will just go read our story. But here’s a quick primer.
https://ter.li/hlbm9cre?mc_cid=6b156c1050&mc_eid=UNIQID

Our focus was the growing “virtual wall” of more than 800 surveillance towers that the US has spent 25 years and billions of dollars building, with political support from both parties.

Increasingly powered by AI, it promises to make the border more secure by enabling faster responses to crossings, and even to save the lives of migrants.

But last summer I was looking at a map (https://ter.li/2z940xqs?mc_cid=6b156c1050&mc_eid=UNIQID) of these towers while also seeing some headlines about people who had recently died of heatstroke crossing the border. On a whim, I compared the locations of deaths in New Mexico with the locations of towers and noticed something peculiar: Some of the deaths had occurred right near the towers. Why couldn’t such sophisticated surveillance catch people before they died? And what else is it missing?

That sent us on a journey to compile data on nearly 4,000 migrant deaths and map them against generations of these surveillance towers. It’s the first analysis to do so. We also spoke to more than 45 sources, from Border Patrol agents to volunteers to presidential advisors. I took trips to multiple sections of the border, shadowing search and rescue groups in the New Mexico desert and going on ride-alongs with sheriffs in south Texas.

The resulting story shows that the ongoing humanitarian crisis at the border, which has gone on for decades, is unfolding more visibly than previously understood. It also reveals flaws in the virtual wall just as it’s set to get an unprecedented expansion—Customs and Border Protection (CBP) plans to spend $1 billion to triple the number of towers by 2034.

But I think it also says something about AI.

The fear of AI discussed often today is a cataclysmic one. At its core is a worry AI might get so powerful that humans cease to be able to control it.

But reading our investigation, you might come away with an almost opposite fear: AI can make a complex problem look simpler than it is—and make it harder to tell whether technology is actually working.

CBP’s journey with AI started earlier than most—it was piloting autonomous surveillance towers from the defense tech company Anduril in 2018. The pitch was that these towers could watch the border and alert agents only if they needed to act.

The agency started to make bolder promises about this autonomous technology than any other tech that came before it. Having AI watch the border would mean fewer agents monitoring video feeds, more agents in the field, better information about what they were walking into—and even migrant lives saved.

By many accounts, the autonomy helped: Agents we spoke with said the technology would alert them to people crossing before they saw them. The problem, as our investigation details, is that sometimes these promises were unmet with tragic consequences. Sometimes the technology failed to detect. Sometimes agents failed to respond.

It was hardly the first technology with flaws. But the more people I spoke with, the more it seemed that the promise of humanlike perception had raised expectations for what it could do to the point that the agency felt less compelled—or even less able—to measure its performance. We estimate that more than 110 people have died near the agency’s autonomous systems (along with others who died near legacy systems without AI capability), but we found that when a body is discovered, CBP does not formally investigate whether surveillance should have detected someone or, if they were detected, why agents didn’t reach them before they died.

So yes, fear the drone swarms and bioweapons and agentic cybercrime, of course. But keep an eye on another side of AI risk: the possibility that misplaced faith in AI’s capabilities lowers the standards of judgment and oversight applied to it.

What’s at stake in AI’s trillion-dollar gamble

A handful of so-called hyperscalers—Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, and Oracle—are poised to spend about $750 billion this year on building new data centers, growing to $1.1 trillion through 2027. The reality is that it’s a giant gamble. This story by my colleague David Rotman explains what it would take for it to pay off (and what happens if it doesn’t). (MIT Technology Review (https://ter.li/9jjnx1kz?mc_cid=6b156c1050&mc_eid=UNIQID) )

An AI hallucination nearly triggered a US military operation

An intelligence report this spring in the midst of US strikes on Iran immediately set off alarm bells: It claimed a Chinese ship in the Middle East was transporting components of a nuclear weapons program. The US military got planes into the air to respond and started readying personnel to board the ship. But when officials dug deeper, they found it was “entirely false.” (CNN (https://ter.li/sqvsv493?mc_cid=6b156c1050&mc_eid=UNIQID) )

Gemini has carried out Google’s first known AI hack

Back in May, Google’s Gemini model accessed the internet and hacked other companies during tests run by a small Israeli cybersecurity startup called Irregular, which was also involved in similar incidents disclosed by OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta. Google said it didn’t disclose the hacks at the time because the model ended each intrusion after determining it had accessed a real company’s systems. (WSJ (https://ter.li/29n0c1ha?mc_cid=6b156c1050&mc_eid=UNIQID) )

OpenAI and Microsoft execs agree with AI’s critics

Large language models are built on the “largest theft of labor in human history” and are killing the web, according to executives working on AI at Microsoft and OpenAI. The admissions were made public last Thursday in documents that had been sealed or redacted at the request of the companies, as part of the long-running copyright lawsuit New York Times v. OpenAI.
